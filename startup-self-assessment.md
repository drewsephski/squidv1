# Startup Self-Assessment Workbook

A founder's guide to honestly evaluating your startup across six critical dimensions.

---

## Product & Problem Clarity

These questions test whether you're solving a real problem for real people, or building something nobody needs.

### How did you first discover this problem, and what convinced you it was worth working on?

**Why this matters:** Viable startups usually start with a personal pain, a customer complaint you couldn't ignore, or an observation about broken workflows. Ideas that come from "what if we built..." speculation without grounding in reality tend to fail.

**How to answer:**
- Tell the specific story. Where were you? What happened? What did you feel?
- If you didn't experience it personally, whose pain did you witness directly?
- What was the moment you moved from "that's annoying" to "someone should fix this" to "I'm going to fix this"?

**Red flags:**
- "We saw a market opportunity in the research"
- "Everyone says this is a growing market"
- "We did a survey and people said they'd use it"

---

### Who feels this problem most acutely, and what do they do today without your product?

**Why this matters:** You need to know your ideal customer with uncomfortable specificity. "Small businesses" or "developers" isn't enough. The person who feels the pain most acutely is your wedge—they'll tolerate your rough edges because their need is urgent.

**How to answer:**
- Name a specific person (or persona) with demographics, role, company type, and situation
- Describe their workaround today in painful detail. What tools do they string together? What manual steps do they take? Who do they bug to get things done?
- The workaround reveals the true competition and the urgency of the problem

**Good example:**
> "Sarah, operations manager at a 50-person DTC brand, spends 3 hours every Monday morning copying data from Shopify into a Google Sheet, then manually formatting it for her weekly report. She hates this but can't get engineering time to build a proper dashboard."

---

### What's the "day in the life" before your product and after your product?

**Why this matters:** This reveals whether you truly understand your user's workflow or are just feature-building. It also shows the magnitude of improvement you deliver.

**How to answer:**
- Walk through a specific hour-by-hour (or task-by-task) scenario before your solution
- Show the same workflow after your solution
- Quantify the difference: time saved, errors reduced, frustration eliminated, new capabilities unlocked
- Be honest about what doesn't change—overclaiming destroys credibility

**Template:**
| Before | After | Impact |
|--------|-------|--------|
| [Task 1] | [How you change it] | [Time/error/quality delta] |
| [Task 2] | [How you change it] | [Time/error/quality delta] |

---

### What's something non-obvious you've learned about your users that outsiders usually get wrong?

**Why this matters:** This is your "unfair understanding." If outsiders could guess your insights, they could compete with you. Non-obvious learning comes from deep user interaction, not desk research.

**How to answer:**
- Identify a counterintuitive pattern you've observed after talking to dozens of users
- Explain what most people assume vs. what you've learned is true
- Connect this to product decisions you've made

**Examples:**
- "Everyone assumes users want more features, but our users actually want fewer choices—decision fatigue is their real pain"
- "People think this is a technical problem, but it's actually a political problem between teams"
- "Users say they want X, but we've learned they actually need Y—they just don't know how to articulate it"

---

### If your product vanished tomorrow, who would scream the loudest and why?

**Why this matters:** This identifies your true fans and reveals whether you've built actual dependency or just nice-to-have usage.

**How to answer:**
- Name specific people or companies
- Explain exactly what breaks in their workflow without you
- Quantify the pain: what does it cost them in time, money, or missed opportunity?
- If the answer is "nobody would scream," you have a problem

## Market & Strategy

These questions test whether you have a clear-eyed view of your market, timing, and path to winning.

### Why is now the right time for this company to exist?

**Why this matters:** Most good ideas fail because the timing is wrong. You need a specific, defensible reason why 2024/2025 is different from 2019 or 2029 for your specific problem.

**How to answer:**
- Identify the specific shift(s) that make this viable now: technology, regulation, behavior, cost structure
- Explain why it wasn't possible/viable 3-5 years ago
- Explain why the window won't close (or why you'll have momentum) in 3-5 years

**Common timing drivers:**
- Technology shift: LLMs became viable, mobile reached ubiquity, cloud costs dropped
- Behavioral shift: Remote work normalized, creators became mainstream, AI acceptance grew
- Regulatory shift: GDPR created need, open banking enabled access
- Cost shift: APIs became cheap enough, hardware costs dropped, compute became abundant

---

### How big can this get if things go well, and what has to be true for that to happen?

**Why this matters:** You need a theory of victory. This isn't about making up TAM numbers—it's about articulating the expansion path from your starting point.

**How to answer:**
- Start with your wedge: what's the realistic market for your initial product?
- Map the expansion path: adjacent users, use cases, geographies, product lines
- List the specific things that must be true for each expansion to work
- Be honest about dependencies: "If AI keeps improving..." or "If we can crack enterprise sales..."

**Structure:**
```
Year 1-2: [Wedge market, $X if we hit Y% penetration]
Year 3-5: [Adjacent expansion, $X if we add Z use cases]
Year 5+: [Full vision, $X if we become the platform for...]
Critical assumptions: [List 3-5 things that must be true]
```

---

### What are the strongest alternatives (including "do nothing" and spreadsheets), and why do people still pick them?

**Why this matters:** Understanding why you lose reveals where your product is weak and what you're actually competing against. "Do nothing" and "use a spreadsheet" are often your biggest competitors.

**How to answer:**
- List all alternatives, including DIY solutions and inertia
- For each, explain why a rational person would choose it over you
- Identify the specific scenarios where each alternative wins
- Show how you're narrowing the scenarios where alternatives make sense

**Alternative categories:**
- Direct competitors (similar products)
- Indirect competitors (different approach, same outcome)
- DIY solutions (spreadsheets, scripts, manual processes)
- Status quo (doing nothing, living with the pain)
- Legacy solutions (enterprise software they're already paying for)

---

### In five years, what would you need to be true for this to look like a clear success?

**Why this matters:** This forces long-term thinking and reveals whether you have conviction. It also shows what you might need to start building toward now.

**How to answer:**
- Define "clear success" for your specific context (market position, revenue, impact, etc.)
- Work backward: what would need to be true in year 4? Year 3?
- Identify the key inflection points and metrics
- Be specific: "dominant player in X" is less useful than "the default choice for Y type of company doing Z"

---

### What's your wedge into the market, and how does it expand over time?

**Why this matters:** You can't win everywhere at once. A clear wedge strategy shows you understand constraint and sequencing.

**How to answer:**
- Define your wedge: specific user segment, use case, geography, or integration point
- Explain why this wedge is winnable for you specifically (not just underserved, but where you have advantages)
- Map the logical expansion: what's next door to your wedge that you can reach once you have a foothold?
- Show how each expansion builds on previous momentum

## Traction & Reality Check

These questions separate what's working from what you hope is working.

### What traction do you have today (users, revenue, retention, engagement), and what are you most proud of?

**Why this matters:** Founders often emphasize vanity metrics. Real traction is users who depend on you, revenue they pay willingly, and engagement that compounds over time.

**How to answer:**
- Lead with the metric that best shows real value creation (often retention or organic usage)
- Include absolute numbers and growth rates
- Explain why you're proud of specific traction: "100 users paying $50/month" vs "10,000 signups"
- Be honest about what's not working yet

**Key metrics to include:**
- Number of active users (with your definition of "active")
- Revenue (ARR/MRR, and growth rate)
- Retention (cohort curves, churn rate)
- Engagement (frequency of use, depth of use)
- Organic growth (referrals, inbound, word of mouth)

---

### Which metrics do you personally look at every week, and why those?

**Why this matters:** Your attention reveals what you believe drives the business. It also shows whether you have a clear model of how your business works.

**How to answer:**
- List the 3-5 metrics you actually check weekly (not the ones you think you should check)
- Explain the causal logic: "I watch X because if X moves, it means Y is working, which leads to Z"
- Show how these metrics connect to your ultimate goals
- Acknowledge if you're still figuring out which metrics matter

**Example:**
> "I watch: (1) Weekly active teams—if this drops, we're not delivering value; (2) Messages sent per team—this predicts retention; (3) New teams from referrals—shows word of mouth is working; (4) Churned teams in past 7 days—early warning for product issues."

---

### What have users paid for so far, and what have they refused to pay for?

**Why this matters:** Payment reveals real preference. What people say they'll pay for and what they actually pay for are different. Understanding refusals shows you where your value proposition is weak.

**How to answer:**
- Catalog what users have paid for, including surprising things
- Note what you've tried to charge for that didn't work
- Analyze patterns: what do successful payments have in common?
- Show how you're learning from refusals (not just ignoring them)

---

### What's the most surprising way people are using the product right now?

**Why this matters:** Users always surprise you. These surprises reveal product-market fit you didn't design for and potential new markets.

**How to answer:**
- Describe specific, concrete examples of unexpected usage
- Explain what the users are trying to accomplish (their job to be done)
- Show how you're responding: leaning in, ignoring, or adapting
- Connect to product strategy if relevant

---

### Where are you seeing the most organic pull (inbound interest, usage, or referrals)?

**Why this matters:** Organic pull is the best signal of product-market fit. If you have to push for every sale or signup, you haven't found it yet.

**How to answer:**
- Quantify organic channels: what percentage of new users/revenue comes unsolicited?
- Describe specific examples of organic behavior (unsolicited referrals, unprompted usage)
- Explain why you think this organic pull is happening
- Show where you're investing to amplify it

## Team & Moat

These questions test whether you have durable advantages beyond "we work hard."

### Why are you and your team uniquely suited to build this?

**Why this matters:** Competent teams can copy most ideas. You need specific advantages: unique insight, technical edge, distribution, or lived experience with the problem.

**How to answer:**
- Identify specific, hard-to-replicate advantages (not generic traits like "we're passionate")
- Connect team background to the specific challenges of this business
- Show evidence: what have you already done that others couldn't easily copy?
- Be honest about gaps and how you're addressing them

**Types of unique fit:**
- Technical: proprietary research, rare expertise, speed advantage
- Domain: deep experience with the customer/problem
- Distribution: existing audience, unique partnerships, community access
- Network: relationships that open doors others can't access

---

### What have you done that would be hardest for a competitor to copy?

**Why this matters:** This is your moat—how you plan to stay ahead as competitors emerge. Most moats are built over time, not launched with.

**How to answer:**
- Identify moat elements you've already built: data network effects, switching costs, brand, integrations
- Explain what makes each hard to copy (time, money, coordination problems)
- Show how you're actively widening the moat
- Be realistic: what could competitors copy quickly if they tried?

---

### What are the biggest execution risks over the next 12-18 months?

**Why this matters:** Founders who can't name their risks are either naive or hiding something. Risk awareness enables preparation and contingency planning.

**How to answer:**
- List the top 3-5 things that could kill or seriously wound the company
- For each, explain: (1) why it's a risk, (2) how you're monitoring it, (3) what you'll do if it materializes
- Balance technical, market, team, and financial risks
- Show you're taking action to mitigate, not just listing and hoping

**Common risk categories:**
- Technical: can we actually build this? At scale?
- Market: will customers actually buy? In sufficient numbers?
- Team: can we hire/retain the talent we need?
- Financial: will we run out of money before reaching milestones?
- Competitive: will a big player or well-funded startup crush us?

---

### What's the hardest decision you've had to make so far, and how did you make it?

**Why this matters:** This reveals your decision-making process under uncertainty. It also shows what you've already faced and how you've grown.

**How to answer:**
- Choose a decision with real tradeoffs (not obvious right answers)
- Explain the context, options, and stakes
- Show your decision process: what information did you gather? Who did you talk to? What principles did you apply?
- Share the outcome and what you learned

**Examples:**
- Firing a co-founder or early hire
- Pivoting away from a product direction you'd invested in
- Turning down a customer or market segment
- Raising at a lower valuation to get the right investor
- Saying no to an acquisition offer

---

### If you had to 10x the company with your current team, what would break first?

**Why this matters:** This reveals the constraints and bottlenecks in your current setup. Every team has a breaking point—knowing yours enables planning.

**How to answer:**
- Honestly assess what would fail if demand or scale suddenly 10x'd
- Identify specific bottlenecks: technical infrastructure, customer support, sales capacity, founder bandwidth
- Show you're thinking about this proactively (not just hoping it doesn't happen)
- Explain what you'd do to address the likely failure points

## Business Model & Runway

These questions test financial viability and resource discipline.

### How do you make money now, and how do you plan to make money later?

**Why this matters:** Many startups launch with one model but plan to evolve. The path from now to later needs to be coherent.

**How to answer:**
- Describe current revenue model with specifics: pricing, packaging, who pays
- Explain the unit economics: CAC, LTV, gross margin
- Map the evolution: how does the model expand or shift over time?
- Show how current pricing/positioning sets up future monetization

**Evolution patterns:**
- Single product → platform with multiple products
- Self-serve → sales-led with enterprise tier
- Transaction fees → SaaS subscription
- Free → freemium → enterprise

---

### What does your ideal customer contract look like (ACV, sales cycle, who signs)?

**Why this matters:** Your ideal contract reveals where you're positioned in the market and what kind of sales motion you're building.

**How to answer:**
- Define ACV (annual contract value) and why that number makes sense for your product
- Describe the sales cycle: how long from first touch to closed deal?
- Identify the buyer: title, department, what they care about
- Show how you're optimizing for this ideal contract (and why)

---

### What's your current runway, and what are the milestones for the next raise?

**Why this matters:** Running out of money is how startups die. You need a clear plan for extending or raising before the tank hits empty.

**How to answer:**
- State current cash position and monthly burn
- Calculate runway in months (be conservative on revenue assumptions)
- Define specific milestones that would make a next raise successful
- Show contingency plans if milestones take longer or market conditions change

**Structure:**
```
Cash: $X
Monthly burn: $Y
Runway: Z months (with/without revenue growth assumptions)
Milestones for next raise: [Specific, measurable targets]
Contingency if fundraising is hard: [Cost reduction, revenue acceleration, etc.]
```

---

### If you had to get to profitability without raising more capital, what would you change?

**Why this matters:** This tests your discipline and creativity. It's also a real scenario many startups face when fundraising gets hard.

**How to answer:**
- Model a path to profitability with current cash
- Identify specific cuts: headcount, marketing spend, office costs, founder salaries
- Explain how you'd accelerate revenue: pricing changes, focus on high-margin segments, sales focus
- Show the timeline and key decision points
- Be honest about whether this path is viable or would kill growth

---

### Where are you deliberately not spending money right now?

**Why this matters:** Resource discipline reveals strategic clarity. Startups die by spending on too many things, not too few.

**How to answer:**
- Identify areas where you're intentionally under-investing
- Explain the rationale: not a priority, not proven, can be added later
- Show what you're saying no to (marketing campaigns, hires, features, markets)
- Demonstrate that you're focused on the few things that matter most

**Examples:**
- "We're not spending on paid marketing—all growth is organic while we nail product"
- "We're not hiring sales yet—founders are selling to learn what works"
- "We're not building mobile yet—web is 90% of our usage, mobile can come later"
- "We're not in Europe yet—US market has 5 years of growth ahead"

## Honest "Sharp Edge" Questions

These force uncomfortable truths and reveal how you think about tradeoffs.

### What's the strongest argument against your company succeeding?

**Why this matters:** If you can't articulate the best case against yourself, you haven't thought hard enough. Every good idea has serious risks.

**How to answer:**
- State the strongest, most coherent argument against your success
- Don't strawman—make it as compelling as you can
- Show that you understand and respect this argument
- Explain what you're doing to address it (or why you think it's wrong)

---

### What have your smartest critics told you that you still think about?

**Why this matters:** Smart critics often see things you miss. How you engage with criticism reveals your intellectual honesty and adaptability.

**How to answer:**
- Quote specific feedback from people you respect
- Explain why it stuck with you (what truth does it contain?)
- Show how it influenced your thinking or actions
- If you rejected it, explain your reasoning

---

### Which assumption, if wrong, would most change your strategy?

**Why this matters:** This reveals your key leverage points. Knowing your critical assumptions helps you test them early and watch for disconfirming evidence.

**How to answer:**
- Identify the 2-3 assumptions your strategy rests on
- For each, explain what would happen if it were wrong
- Show how you're testing or monitoring these assumptions
- Have contingency plans for if they prove false

**Common critical assumptions:**
- "Users will pay for this once they try it"
- "This technology will keep improving at current rates"
- "Our CAC will stay under $X as we scale"
- "This market will consolidate around a single winner"
- "Enterprises will adopt this bottom-up"

---

### What's a painful truth about your business that you rarely put in the deck?

**Why this matters:** Every business has ugly truths. Acknowledging them shows maturity and builds trust. Hiding them destroys credibility when they inevitably surface.

**How to answer:**
- Identify something true but uncomfortable about your business
- Explain why it's painful (threatens narrative, worries investors, makes you look bad)
- Show how you're addressing it or why it doesn't kill the opportunity
- Don't pick something trivial—go for real discomfort

**Examples:**
- "Our biggest customer is 40% of revenue and could leave"
- "We're dependent on OpenAI and have no differentiation if they compete"
- "Our technical founder is leaving in 6 months"
- "We've pivoted twice and still don't have clear PMF"
- "The market is crowded and we're the 10th player"

---

### If you had only 3 months of cash left, what would you do differently starting tomorrow?

**Why this matters:** This reveals your true priorities. What you cut and what you keep shows what you actually believe is essential.

**How to answer:**
- Describe the immediate changes: cuts, focus shifts, revenue acceleration
- Explain the decision criteria: what's truly essential vs. nice to have
- Show the timeline: what happens in week 1, month 1, month 3
- Be honest about what's at risk and what you're betting on

**Common moves:**
- Cut all non-essential spend (marketing, events, nice-to-have tools)
- Focus team on single highest-conviction initiative
- Founders take over sales/support to reduce burn
- Attempt revenue acceleration: pricing changes, upsell pushes, new customer segments
- Consider acquisition, merger, or acqui-hire options

---

## Using This Workbook

### For Yourself

1. **Set aside 2-3 hours** to work through all sections honestly
2. **Write out full answers**—don't just skim or think about them
3. **Identify the gaps**—which questions made you uncomfortable or were hard to answer?
4. **Take action**—turn weak answers into 30/60/90-day priorities

### For Your Team

1. **Have each co-founder complete independently** before comparing
2. **Discuss differences**—where you disagree often reveals important unspoken assumptions
3. **Align on priorities**—use gaps to set shared goals for the quarter

### For Investors

1. **Prepare concise versions** of each answer (1-2 sentences)
2. **Have supporting details ready** for follow-up questions
3. **Lead with honesty**—acknowledging weaknesses builds more trust than hiding them

---

*Remember: The goal isn't perfect answers—it's honest answers. The gaps you identify are more valuable than the strengths you confirm.*
