# Squid Company Values

> The non-obvious principles that guide how we work, hire, and build.

---

## 1. Cognitive Leverage Over Labor Reduction

**The Belief:** AI's greatest value isn't replacing work — it's reshaping how humans collaborate. We don't measure success by "how much human labor can we eliminate?" We measure by **how much more ambitious our users can be with the same effort.**

**The Story:** A developer using Squid isn't outsourcing their thinking to AI. They're compressing the iteration cycle — trying more ideas faster, recovering from mistakes quicker, maintaining creative momentum. The AI becomes an extension of their intent, not a black box they delegate to.

**In Hiring:** Look for people who get genuinely excited about "making users feel unstoppable." Avoid candidates who talk about AI purely in terms of efficiency metrics or cost savings.

**Day-to-Day:** When evaluating features, ask: "Does this make the user feel like the AI is an extension of their thinking, or like they're supervising a black box?" Prioritize the former.

**Anti-Pattern:** Building features that optimize for "hands-off automation" when users actually want deep collaboration.

---

## 2. Invisible Infrastructure

**The Belief:** The best tools are the ones you forget you're using. AI shouldn't be "another app to check" — it should be infrastructure that fades into the background.

**The Story:** When a developer hits `@` to search the web, summarize a GitHub issue, or generate an image, they shouldn't think "I'm using Squid now." They should think "I'm getting work done." The tool is the conduit, not the destination.

**In Hiring:** Look for engineers who obsess over removing friction. Ask: "Tell me about a time you removed steps from a workflow." The best answers won't be about adding features — they'll be about deleting them.

**Day-to-Day:** Default to "no" on feature requests that add UI complexity. Say yes to features that reduce clicks, remove context-switching, or eliminate "app-hopping."

**Anti-Pattern:** Creating a "feature-rich" product that requires users to learn a new interface. If users need a tutorial to be productive, we've failed.

---

## 3. Everyone Ships Growth

**The Belief:** Building great product isn't enough — every person here is responsible for the business growing. Growth isn't a department; it's a behavior.

**The Story:** The engineer who notices a competitor's feature launch and suggests a response in Slack. The designer who suggests a pricing experiment. The community manager who notices a user struggling and escalates it as a product insight. Everyone has permission — and obligation — to think like an owner.

**In Hiring:** Ask candidates: "Tell me about a time you did something outside your job description that helped the business." If they can't think of one, they won't fit here.

**Day-to-Day:** Celebrate when someone identifies a growth opportunity, even if the idea doesn't work. Celebrate the behavior of "seeing the business as yours." Default response to ideas: "Try it and tell us what happens."

**Anti-Pattern:** "That's not my job" thinking. If you see something broken — in product, marketing, support — and you don't at least flag it, you're not aligned with this value.

---

## 4. Document the Why

**The Belief:** Decisions are more valuable than code. When someone works alone at 2am, they should leave breadcrumbs of intent, not just implementation.

**The Story:** A developer adds a mysterious 5-second delay to a database query. Six months later, a new engineer sees the comment: "Adding artificial delay here — without it, users spam the API thinking it's not working." The delay stays. If the comment had only said "sleep(5000)," the next engineer would have "optimized" it away and broken the UX.

**In Hiring:** Give candidates a code snippet with unclear intent. Ask them to write the comment explaining why it exists. You're looking for someone who sees the human context, not just the technical mechanics.

**Day-to-Day:** PR descriptions must explain the problem being solved, not just what changed. Meeting notes capture decisions and trade-offs, not just action items. Onboarding docs explain "why we do it this way" so new hires can question it intelligently.

**Anti-Pattern:** "The code is self-documenting." No, it's not. Your future self — and your teammates — deserve your thinking, not just your keystrokes.

---

## 5. Celebrate in Public

**The Belief:** Wins that aren't shared don't compound. A successful launch, a clever workaround, a user success story — these need to be public to create momentum.

**The Story:** A user forks Squid and builds a custom medical research tool. We don't just thank them privately — we write a case study, tweet about it, invite them to share at our community meetup. Their win becomes our social proof. Their story attracts the next 100 users.

**In Hiring:** Look for people who naturally share their work. Ask about their proudest professional moment — do they light up talking about it? Do they share learnings, not just achievements?

**Day-to-Day:** Default to public over private communication. Ship a feature? Post in Discord. Fix a bug? Tweet the before/after. A user says something nice? Ask if you can share it. Every interaction is marketing if you're building in public.

**Anti-Pattern:** Building quietly until something is "perfect." The perfect launch that never happens is worse than the messy launch that teaches you something.

---

## Operating with These Values

**Hiring Decisions:** Use these as a rubric. Score candidates 1-5 on each value. Anyone below a 3 on "Cognitive Leverage" or "Everyone Ships Growth" is a no-hire, regardless of technical skill.

**Feedback Language:** Reference values directly. "This decision doesn't feel aligned with our value of Invisible Infrastructure — we're adding friction here." Or: "Love how you documented the why in that PR — that's exactly what we need."

**Annual Review:** Revisit these values yearly. They're not commandments; they're hypotheses. If one stops serving us, we change it. But we change it explicitly, not by ignoring it.

---

## Distinctive by Design

These values are meant to be polarizing:

- A developer who wants AI to "just handle everything" will read value #1 and realize Squid isn't for them. Good.
- Someone who thinks growth is "the founders' job" will self-select out. Good.
- An engineer who hates writing comments will feel uncomfortable here. Good.

We're not building for everyone. We're building for the people who read these values and think: **"Finally, a team that gets it."**

That resonance is the whole point.
